# Investment Efficiency Shocks in a Real Business Cycle Framework

## Overview

This project builds a calibrated RBC/DSGE-style model with TFP and investment-efficiency shocks to study investment volatility and macroeconomic shock transmission.

The project combines empirical time-series analysis using U.S. macroeconomic data with model-based simulations in Matlab/Dynare.

## Research Question

How do investment-efficiency shocks affect macroeconomic aggregates such as output, consumption, investment, capital, and the real return to capital?

## Coursework Scope and Independent Research Extensions

This project originated from an undergraduate macroeconomics coursework exercise on the implementation of a Real Business Cycle (RBC) model with investment-efficiency shocks.

The core coursework requirement was primarily computational: students were expected to formulate the model, calibrate the relevant parameters, implement the system in Dynare, and obtain a valid numerical solution together with the associated impulse response functions. In other words, a correctly specified and successfully executed model was sufficient to satisfy the minimum requirements of the assignment.

The theoretical components used for this baseline implementation—including household optimisation, firm first-order conditions, market clearing, steady-state derivation, calibration, stochastic TFP and investment-efficiency processes, and Dynare-based simulation—were all part of the material introduced in the course.

However, the submitted project goes beyond this baseline implementation. Rather than treating successful model execution as the endpoint of the exercise, I extended the coursework into a research-oriented evaluation of whether the mechanisms generated by the model are quantitatively consistent with observed U.S. business-cycle dynamics.

The distinction between the original coursework and the additional research components is summarised below.

### Motivation for the Extensions

The additional components were not introduced simply to increase the technical complexity of the project. Each extension was motivated by a limitation of what could be learned from the baseline coursework alone.

#### 1. Structural VAR as an Empirical Benchmark

A model-generated impulse response shows how variables behave conditional on the assumptions imposed by the theoretical model. It does not, by itself, establish whether comparable dynamic relationships are present in the data.

I therefore estimated a Structural VAR using U.S. output, investment, and consumption data to construct an empirical benchmark for the model-generated impulse responses.

The purpose of this extension is to move from the internal question

> *How does an investment-efficiency shock propagate within the RBC model?*

to the empirical question

> *Are the dynamic responses implied by the model broadly consistent with those observed in U.S. macroeconomic data?*

The SVAR is therefore used as an empirical reference point rather than as a substitute for the structural model. It first characterises how macroeconomic variables move in the data, while the RBC model is subsequently used to provide an economic interpretation of the underlying transmission mechanism.

#### 2. Business-Cycle Moment Comparison with U.S. Data

Impulse responses are useful for studying the direction and persistence of responses to particular shocks, but they do not establish whether the model reproduces the overall quantitative properties of the business cycle.

For this reason, I additionally compared the simulated volatility of output, consumption, and investment with corresponding moments calculated from U.S. data.

This extension addresses the question

> *Can the calibrated model reproduce not only the direction of macroeconomic responses, but also the relative magnitude of fluctuations observed in the data?*

This comparison is particularly important because the empirical motivation for introducing the investment-efficiency shock is the unusually high volatility of investment relative to output.

#### 3. Diagnosing the Investment-Volatility Mismatch

The addition of an investment-efficiency shock increases the responsiveness and absolute volatility of investment. However, this does not necessarily imply that the model provides a better quantitative explanation of observed business-cycle dynamics.

The project therefore explicitly examines whether the additional shock resolves the investment-volatility puzzle rather than assuming that introducing an additional disturbance automatically improves the model.

The relevant research question is

> *Does introducing a theoretically plausible investment-efficiency shock actually resolve the empirical deficiency that motivated the extension?*

The results show that the answer is only partial. Investment becomes more responsive, but the model remains unable to jointly reproduce the overall level and relative magnitude of volatility observed in U.S. data.

The remaining discrepancy is therefore treated as an economic result rather than merely a calibration or implementation problem.

#### 4. From Calibration to Structural Validation

The remaining model-data mismatch motivates a broader methodological question.

A calibrated model may be internally coherent, reproduce selected steady-state relationships, and generate economically interpretable impulse responses without necessarily providing the correct structural explanation of observed fluctuations.

The project therefore extends the analysis beyond calibration itself and considers the distinction between calibration, empirical fit, and structural validity.

This extension asks

> *To what extent should successful calibration or improved quantitative fit be interpreted as evidence in favour of the underlying economic mechanism?*

The purpose is to distinguish the ability of a model to generate plausible outcomes from the stronger claim that its structural mechanism provides a reliable explanation of the data.

#### 5. Critical Evaluation of the RBC Framework

Finally, the remaining discrepancies are used to evaluate the limitations of the underlying frictionless RBC framework.

Rather than treating model-data differences solely as parameter-selection problems, the analysis considers whether they reflect omitted economic mechanisms that are not captured by the benchmark model.

The final question is therefore

> *What can the model explain, what does it fail to explain, and what do these failures imply about the structure of the model itself?*

This shifts the project from a computational implementation exercise toward a broader exercise in quantitative model evaluation.

### Research Logic

The original coursework can be summarised as:

`model derivation → calibration → Dynare implementation → simulation`

The independent research extensions broaden this sequence to:

`model implementation → empirical benchmark → quantitative model-data comparison → diagnosis of model mismatch → evaluation of structural limitations`

These additions were therefore not included as separate technical exercises. They were introduced sequentially to answer increasingly desmanding questions about the model: first whether the proposed mechanism operates internally, then whether it resembles the data, then whether it reproduces the magnitude of observed fluctuations, and finally what any remaining model-data discrepancy implies for the explanatory power and limitations of the RBC framework.



## Methods

- Processed U.S. quarterly macroeconomic data from FRED, 1970–2024.
- Visualised quarterly growth rates of output, consumption, and investment.
- Estimated a Structural VAR with Cholesky identification to obtain empirical impulse responses.
- Built a calibrated RBC/DSGE-style model with TFP and investment-efficiency shocks.
- Simulated impulse response functions using Matlab/Dynare.
- Compared simulated business-cycle moments with U.S. macroeconomic data.
- Discussed calibration versus estimation and the limitations of moment-matching for structural reliability.
